## CONNECTICUT: OFFER (rests on Connecticut's arbitration act and, as to the anti-waiver rule, federal law)

**Reason.** Connecticut's Revised Uniform Arbitration Act (RUAA) governs agreements made on or after October 1, 2018, and makes a pre-dispute agreement in a record "valid, enforceable and irrevocable". It has no consumer, residential, real-property or summary-process exclusion and no notice or format rule. Its only relevant non-waivable term is the right to a lawyer, which clause (c) keeps. The lease statute's possible blocker is § 47a-4(a)(1). It bars waiving rights "under any section of the general statutes" unless that section expressly allows waiver, and it could be read to reach the civil jury right in § 52-215. That is a general anti-waiver rule that would reach arbitration only through arbitration's defining feature, giving up a jury. Kindred says the FAA displaces exactly that kind of rule. Clause (c) keeps every remedy, and summary process has no jury right and stays in court under (b)(1). The penalty test is met. Section 47a-4(b) makes a prohibited provision only "unenforceable", and no Connecticut statute attaches damages, a multiple of rent, a fine, or AG or criminal enforcement to including or enforcing a prohibited lease term. The library's `edu-no-knowing-use-penalty-ct` reached the same conclusion. The one caveat is CUTPA. It is a general unfair-practices statute with actual and punitive damages, and `edu-no-knowing-use-penalty-ct` already flags as an inference, not a statute, that using unenforceable terms might be a CUTPA violation. That matches the treatment of Hawaii (general UDAP law, offered), so it does not trigger HOLD under the rule as written. The owner may want to weigh it.

## DELAWARE: HOLD

**Reason.** Delaware's Uniform Arbitration Act validates pre-dispute agreements and has no consumer or lease exclusion. The Court of Chancery applies the Act only when an agreement specifically names it, and otherwise applies the FAA standard. The clause's fallback ("the arbitration law of the state") does not name the Act, so the FAA standard would govern either way. The block is the Landlord-Tenant Code. A lease may not provide that the tenant "Agrees to waive or forego rights or remedies under this Code" (25 Del. C. § 5301(a)(1)), and a term that conflicts with the Code and is not expressly authorized is unenforceable (§ 5101(a)). Read broadly, a clause that moves Code money claims from court to an arbitrator could be such a waiver. The Code's own jury right (§ 5713) attaches to chapter 57 summary possession actions, which the clause leaves in court, so the direct jury-waiver concern is small. The anti-waiver rule is still the kind of rule Kindred displaces as applied to arbitration, so the clause probably survives on the merits. It fails the owner's penalty test, though. Section 5301(b) gives the tenant 3 months' rent if the landlord "attempts to enforce provisions of a rental agreement known by the landlord to be prohibited", and a motion to compel arbitration is an attempt to enforce. The trigger is knowing enforcement rather than mere inclusion, which is narrower than in Maryland or Massachusetts. It is still a multiple-of-rent penalty over prohibited lease terms, and the anti-waiver list could be read to reach this clause, so the rule says HOLD. Separately, § 5111 makes a lease provision "providing for the recovery of attorneys' fees by either party in any suit, action or proceeding" unenforceable. Clause (c) only preserves fees a court could award, so it probably does not engage § 5111, but this is worth noting if Delaware is revisited.

## MARYLAND: HOLD

**Reason.** Maryland's arbitration act validates pre-dispute agreements and has no consumer, lease or real-property exclusion; its only exclusion is employer-employee agreements. The Real Property Article is the problem. A landlord "may not use a lease or form of lease containing any provision that" has the tenant waive "any right or remedy provided by applicable law" (§ 8-208(d)(2)) or "waive the right to a jury trial" (§ 8-208(d)(4)), and a primary-residence lease jury waiver is "invalid and unenforceable" (§ 8-603(a)). These are the jury-protecting rules Kindred says the FAA displaces as applied to arbitration, so the clause might well survive on the merits. The owner's rule still requires HOLD, because § 8-208(g)(2) attaches liability to merely tendering a lease that contains a prohibited provision: "actual damages incurred as a reason thereof, including reasonable attorney's fees", with no knowledge requirement. The Consumer Protection Act also reaches leases of consumer realty (Com. Law § 13-303) and carries AG civil penalties (§ 13-410). I did not find any provision that makes a violation of Real Prop. § 8-208 a per se CPA violation, and § 13-301(14) lists Title 13, other Commercial Law subtitles and certain other articles, not RP § 8-208. The § 8-208(g)(2) exposure alone is enough for HOLD.

## MASSACHUSETTS: HOLD

**Reason.** G.L. c. 251, § 1 validates pre-dispute arbitration provisions and excludes only collective bargaining agreements. Chapter 186, § 15F voids any residential lease term "whereby the tenant agrees to waive his right to trial by jury in any subsequent litigation with the landlord". An arbitration clause could be read as that kind of term, and it is the Kindred-type rule the FAA would likely displace as applied to arbitration. Massachusetts attaches heavy enforcement to unfair or deceptive practices: actual damages or $25, doubled or trebled for willful or knowing violations, plus attorney's fees (c. 93A, § 9(3), (4)), and AG injunctions and civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation (c. 93A, § 4). The link from "including an unlawful lease term" to chapter 93A is the Attorney General's regulation 940 CMR 3.17(3)(a), quoted in the library's `edu-unlawful-lease-term-liability-ma`. That regulation is published on mass.gov, which is not one of the allowed sources, so I did not confirm it here and that point is HOLD by rule. Even without it, the combination of a lease-term ban that could reach the clause (§ 15F) with multiple-damages and AG exposure under the consumer statute meets the HOLD test. Chapter 186, §§ 14, 15 and 15B add waiver-void rules for quiet enjoyment, exculpation and deposits. Possession and deposit claims are carved out of the clause, and clause (c) keeps all remedies, but those rules add to the exposure if the clause were read as a waiver.

**Education row (proposed)**
- id: `edu-binding-arbitration-ma`
- title: Why the Library Has No Arbitration Clause for Massachusetts
- content_type LANDLORD_EDUCATION; rule_type CONSTRAINED; topic_key binding-arbitration
- bodyText (210 words):

> Massachusetts's arbitration act makes a provision in a written contract to arbitrate later disputes valid, enforceable and irrevocable except on grounds that would undo any contract (Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 251, § 1). But a residential lease term by which the tenant agrees to waive the right to a jury trial in any later litigation with you is against public policy and void (Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 186, § 15F), and an arbitration clause takes the disputes it covers away from a jury. Putting an unlawful term in a rental agreement is also treated as an unfair or deceptive practice under the Attorney General's consumer protection rules. A tenant injured by one can recover actual damages or $25, whichever is greater, two to three times that if the violation was willful or knowing, plus attorney's fees (Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A, § 9(3), (4)), and the Attorney General can seek civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation (ch. 93A, § 4). Federal law may override the jury rule as applied to arbitration, but a court would decide that only after the lease was in use, and Massachusetts case law was not reviewed. Because guessing wrong can mean multiplied damages, this library does not offer an arbitration clause for Massachusetts.
