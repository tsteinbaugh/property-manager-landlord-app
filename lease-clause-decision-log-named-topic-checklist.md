### Methodology lessons added

1. **Read every section of a multi-section act, especially the one addressed to your own audience.** The CO-alarm error came from reading §§76-603/604/605 (construction and sellers) and stopping before §76-606 (landlords). Four of this session's findings share this single root — smoke detector, §81-5,142, holdover/possession, CO alarms.
2. **A partial quotation from a secondary compilation is not the section.** An NCSL summary quoting only §81-5,142(1) led *this re-audit* to invent a coverage gap that subsections (2) and (3) closed. Caught only because the row was parked at `NEEDS_REVIEW` rather than shipped — over-include-and-flag caught the auditor's own error.
3. **Time-based measures and multipliers are not interchangeable.** "Three months' periodic rent" ≠ "3× periodic rent" outside a monthly tenancy. Paraphrasing a damages formula loses the distinction; quote the statute's construction.
4. **A prohibited-provisions section may not be the complete list.** See §76-1413(9).
5. **When the same error shape appears twice in one session, stop and get primary text.** The CO-alarm suspicion was flagged rather than acted on precisely because §81-5,142 had just gone wrong the same way; §5a.2 was invoked instead of a third search, and the primary text showed the problem was worse than suspected.
6. **A logged-only absence is invisible to future canvasses.** The all-in-pricing row was resolved in this checklist and never entered the library. Confirmed absences need CSV rows, not prose.
7. **The checklist's own main tables can go stale while the CSV is corrected.** The 49-row re-verification found four NE column entries still carrying errors this session had already fixed in the library — the holdover and possession-delay `3×` figures, the 'tracks federal only' protected-class entry, and the carbon-monoxide 'only new construction' scope. Because the checklist *is* the canvass instrument, a future state would have inherited wrong Nebraska benchmarks from it while the CSV said otherwise. Fixing a CSV row is not finishing the job; the checklist entry that sourced it has to move too.
8. **The denominator contains a genuine duplicate topic**, which explains why row-counting and distinct-label-counting diverge rather than merely differing by an off-by-one. 'Returned/dishonored check fee cap' and 'Genuinely new: returned-check-fee cap (candidate, investigated)' are the same subject occupying two counted rows in two sections. A deduplicated count legitimately returns a smaller number than a row count. ### CO — HIGH PRIORITY FLAG, not yet a confirmed error

**C.R.S. § 13-40-107(1) sets a five-tier notice scheme**, not a single figure:

| Tenancy | Notice |
|---|---|
| One year or longer | **91 days** |
| Six months or longer but less than a year | **28 days** |
| One month or longer but less than six months | **21 days** |
| One week or longer but less than one month, or tenancy at will | **3 days** |
| Less than one week | **1 day** |

Both shipped CO clauses state **21 days flat** — the third tier only. If "tenancy" is measured by **duration of occupancy** (which is how CO practitioner material reads it: *"Determine the tenancy length to identify the correct notice period: 21 days (1–6 months), 28 days (6–12 months), 91 days (1+ year)"*), then a Colorado month-to-month tenant of two years is owed **91 days**, and our clause understates it by seventy days.

**Why this is flagged rather than corrected here.** Three reasons, all deliberate:
1. The **duration-of-occupancy vs rental-interval** reading of "a tenancy of…" needs confirmation against primary text and CO case law. The statute is old (L. 1885) with a long amendment history — the 1–6 month tier read *seven days* until 2017.
2. CO carries an **HB24-1098 just-cause overlay** for 12+ month tenancies that interacts with any no-cause non-renewal notice, and both shipped rows are already split into `-exempt` / `-covered` variants because of it.
3. **Editing CO rows from an ND session would violate Addendum E discipline** — a cosmetic repair is one thing, a substantive notice-period rewrite is CO re-verification and belongs in a CO session.

**RESOLVED AND CORRECTED 2026-09-06 (v117), at Taylor's direction** — there being no planned future CO session. Current § 13-40-107 read from primary text as amended by **HB24-1098 (ch. 113, § 5, effective 2024-04-19)**.

**The duration reading is settled by the statute's own words:** § 13-40-107(1) requires notice *"based on the length of the applicable tenancy described in subsection (2)."* HB24-1098 also inserted **"at least"** before each figure — the tiers are floors, not fixed periods.

Both CO clauses corrected. **Three distinct defects, not one:**

1. **Flat 21 days.** Corrected to the full five-tier scheme in both rows. A month-to-month tenant of two years was owed 91 days; the clause understated by seventy.
2. **`-covered` was doubly wrong on its own terms** — even inside its stated "first 12 months" window, months six through twelve require at least 28 days, not 21.
3. **`-covered` had the wrong party.** As amended, § 13-40-107(1) grants the right to *"A landlord of nonresidential real property or a residential premises described in section 38-12-1302(1)(a), (1)(b), (1)(d), (1)(e), or (1)(f) **or a tenant of any property or premises**."* A **covered residential landlord is not in that list**; a tenant of any property is. The section supplies a tenant-side route on a covered property and no landlord-side one. Clause rewritten accordingly.

**Also caught, and separately rowed as `edu-fixed-term-nonrenewal-notice-co`:** the pre-2024 text excused notice *"from or to"* a tenant whose term ended at a time certain. Current § 13-40-107(4) excuses it **only "from a tenant."** The words *"or to"* are gone. **The common landlord assumption that a fixed-term lease simply expires with no notice required is no longer correct in Colorado** — § 13-40-107(1) expressly covers electing "to not renew a fixed term tenancy," and the tiers apply, so declining renewal on a year-plus tenancy takes at least 91 days' notice.

**Left open and deliberately not asserted:** by what route, if any, a covered residential landlord may terminate without cause during months 0–12. § 38-12-1303's just-cause requirement attaches at 12 months, but § 13-40-107(1) does not name covered residential landlords at all. § 38-12-1301 et seq. and § 38-12-1302 were **not read** this session. The clause no longer asserts a landlord no-cause right in that window — the safe direction — but the question is open.

**Scope discipline:** edited from the ND re-audit session at Taylor's express direction. Per Addendum E this is a targeted correction of the notice-period holdings only. **It is not a CO re-audit**, and no other CO holding was reviewed.

### WY and MN — no termination-notice row exists at all

Neither state has any row stating how a periodic tenancy is terminated. This is the **same omission shape** ND exhibited: not a wrong row, an absent one, invisible because this checklist has no generic *"termination notice period"* topic row for a canvass to hit.

MN is the more likely to bite: § 504B.135 scales the notice to *"the interval between the time rent is due or three months, whichever is shorter"* — a rule that is **inherently tenancy-type-scaled**, so a single stated figure could not capture it even if one existed.

### WY and MN — gaps filled (v118)

**WY: confirmed absent, and the absence is the finding.** Wyoming sets **no statutory notice period** for ending a periodic tenancy — not month-to-month, not week-to-week, not a periodic tenancy of a year or more. Evidence is affirmative rather than absence-of-mention: a structured state chart returns "No statute" across all four categories, and Nolo states outright that neither party is required by statute to give notice. The ubiquitous "30 days" is **common-law practice, not statute** — and the common-law rule is itself tenancy-type scaled (one full rental period, terminating at the start of a new period), so a landlord applying "30 days" to a weekly tenancy is wrong by a factor of four. The row says the 30-day figure is non-statutory expressly, and states the rule at practice-level confidence since no WY case fixing it was located.

**MN: the purest example the screen found of why a flat figure cannot work.** § 504B.135 states **no number at all** — notice must be *"at least as long as the interval between the time rent is due or three months, whichever is less."* That is a **formula**. Any row saying "MN requires X days" is wrong for every tenancy whose rent interval isn't X. Note *"whichever is less"* makes three months a **ceiling**, not a floor.

**A repeal caught in the same read:** the section formerly carried a second paragraph giving a **14-day notice to quit** for unpaid rent on a tenancy at will. Struck by 2023 Minn. Laws ch. 52, art. 19, § 97, effective 2024-01-01, applying to leases entered into or renewed on or after that date. The current official text carries no subdivision designations at all, confirming the deletion. **Justia's 2013 and 2016 versions still show the "(a)" designation** — a live K.4 archived-version trap for anyone reading a mirror.

**Open on the MN row, flagged not asserted:** MN case law reportedly fixes a **counting rule** for when the notice takes effect, traced by a secondary source to 1891 authority. Not located in primary form, deliberately omitted from the row body. A landlord could satisfy the length requirement and still serve ineffective notice on timing.

### SD — answer found, routed to the SD session rather than fixed here

**§ 43-32-15** supplies what `edu-tenancy-at-will-termination-sd` does not: a hiring for a term not specified by the parties is deemed renewed unless a party gives notice of intention to terminate *"at least as long before the expiration thereof as the term of the hiring itself, not exceeding one month."* **Scaled to the term, capped at one month** — so a week-to-week SD tenancy takes about a week's notice.

**That is structurally identical to ND's § 47-16-15(1)**, and the reason is visible in the source lines: SD's chapter traces to *CivC 1877* — the Dakota Territory Civil Code, which both Dakotas inherited. **SD and ND leasing chapters are near-parallel by descent.** § 43-32-12 (rent timing: agricultural / lodging / other) mirrors ND § 47-16-20; § 43-32-13 (30-day modification notice, tenant's 15-day response) mirrors ND § 47-16-07 and § 47-16-15(3).

**Directed input to the SD session, which is next:** ND findings are *candidates* for SD and vice versa, but must be verified rather than inherited — the shared ancestry makes assumed transfer especially tempting and especially dangerous. Note one confirmed divergence already: SD gives tenants at will who are active servicemembers **two months' written notice** (§ 43-8-8), where ND has **no state military provision at all**.

SD is also still not a column in this file.

### How to use this section

**Do not fill a cell from a decision log without checking against primary text first.** This rule produced the original SD-only cells, each backed by primary statutory text read during the SD re-audit with the section number in the cell. The table has since been filled for the other six states too, following the same discipline — each cell's own sourcing note (e.g. "verified from wyoleg.gov," "tiered below the other six," or an explicit "not read from primary text in this pass") tells you how much weight that specific cell can bear. A cell filled straight from a prior log's narrative, without this kind of independent verification, would carry that log's confidence — and this project's re-audit standard is that logs are claims to verify, not facts.

**Expect this section to find errors.** Two of the fourteen SD rows above record provisions that were *wrong or missing in the library* until the SD re-audit: the deposit-return deadline had shipped a superseded figure for over two months, and the state reasonable-accommodation duty was absent entirely. Both are universal obligations. That hit rate on the first state canvassed is the argument for running the other six.

**Sequencing note.** These topics are cheap to canvass — each is a single well-known provision in every state — but they are load-bearing, since most appear in `REQUIRED` clauses that ship into every generated lease. They should rank above further exotic-topic discovery.
